# Episode 4 creative brief: the dinosaur asteroid

Ep3 promised: "Next time, something does hit us… sixty-six million years ago… minute by minute."

The trap: the most-watched video on this exact topic is Kurzgesagt's "The Day the Dinosaurs Died – Minute by Minute"
(~22 M views): a god's-eye clock over the whole planet. Doing that again would be a copy, and it would also be our
own Ep1/Ep3 shape (watch the whole system from above, then list the damage).

The asset nobody else has built a video around: **Tanis**, a fossil site in North Dakota, 3,050 km from the crater
(DePalma et al. 2019, PNAS). Paddlefish and sturgeon died there with impact glass in their gills; unaltered glass
from Chicxulub is sealed in amber on the tree trunks; a ≥ 10 m surge buried everything within the first two hours.
The paper's own clock: P waves at 6 min, S waves at 10, surface waves at 13, glass reaching the sky overhead from
13 to 25 min, most of it down within 1–2 h. And our physics adds the twist: **at 3,000 km everything arrives in the
wrong order.** The light comes first, then the ground moves, then glass falls from a clear sky, and the *sound* of
the impact arrives last, two and a half hours later, long after the river has already killed everything in it.

## Story shapes considered
1. **The god's-eye minute clock** over the whole planet (crater, tsunami, fires, winter). Rejected: it is
   Kurzgesagt's video, and our Ep1/Ep3 god's-eye shape.
2. **Race of the messengers:** follow each thing the impact releases (light, quake, glass, air blast, tsunami,
   soot) outward at its own speed. Strong idea, but it keeps cutting between continents, so nobody is ever present.
3. **One riverbank, one clock (chosen):** the camera never leaves one place 3,050 km from the impact, and the
   minutes are counted by what *arrives* there. The race of the messengers is told by their arrival order at a
   single spot. The cast is the river: paddlefish, sturgeon, the forest, the water.
4. **The forensic rewind:** open on the fossil and work backwards clue by clue. Kept as the frame. The open and the
   end are the evidence (the X-ray of a gill full of glass, the bead in amber); everything between is the reconstruction.
5. **Ride the asteroid down** (its point of view, last seconds). Rejected: that is a descent, which is Ep2's shape.

## Chosen
Shape 3 inside the frame of shape 4. A two-hour vigil on one riverbank, counted by arrivals, bracketed by the
evidence that lets us know the minute. The "minute by minute" promise is kept literally: a clock runs for the
whole vigil, and it is the arrivals that move the story forward. It also flips the channel's question: not "what
if", but "how do we know?", with the simulation used to check the fossil's timeline.

## Unlike earlier episodes
- **Story shape:** a single place and a single clock (unity of place), not an approach from space (Ep1, Ep3) and
  not a descent (Ep2). There are no branching fates or endings.
- **Narrator:** third person, present tense, no "you" at all. Ep1–Ep3 all spoke to "you".
- **Point of view:** eye level and underwater in a Cretaceous river. No orbital views of the Solar System, and
  the globe appears only as a small arrival map.
- **Ending:** a forensic close (the glass in amber, matched to the crater's black glass), not "should you worry"
  with the odds.
- **Hook:** a fish breathing in glass in the first seconds, and the question of how the glass got there in 15
  minutes. It is not a countdown or a date.
- **Palette:** fern green, river silt and ember red. Ep2 was amber, Ep3 violet and ice.
- **Sound:** its own sound bed (river ambience, glass rain ticking on water, a silence, then the boom that arrives
  last). It does not reuse the earlier cue library.
- **Thumbnail:** an everyday-scale object holds the cosmic event: a bead of amber with tiny glass spheres inside,
  with the impact glow in the sky reflected in it. No text, no number.

## Honesty notes
- Tanis's interpretation is debated (seiche vs. other surge; exact timing comes from calculation). Say so once, plainly.
- DePalma was investigated over a separate season paper and cleared of fabrication (Univ. of Manchester, 2023); the
  spring timing is supported independently (During et al. 2022, Nature). We only say "probably in spring".
- Sky heating: Goldin & Melosh (2009) give a peak near 6 kW/m², above sunlight for ~25 min; Melosh et al. (1990)
  gave far more; a 2026 dust-blanket study argues higher again. Present it as a range, not a single scary number.

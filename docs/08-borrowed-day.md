# 08 · New format study: "What If You Were ___ for One Day?"

Working name: **BORROWED DAY**. *You borrow someone's life for 24 hours. At midnight, you have to give it back.*

Research date: **22 Oct 2026**. View counts were pulled from YouTube search results that day. Same engine, with the rules from the founder's review of Ep1 applied (see the last section).

---

## 1. Verdict

**The topic is proven, and the plain version of it is already dead.**

- **Demand is proven at the multi-million level.** The "be someone else for a day" wish is one of the most-clicked premises on YouTube.
- **Supply is flooding in.** In the last month, dozens of AI channels have uploaded the exact titles on your list, and almost all of them got **1–100 views**.
- **The title is a commodity.** The winners won on something else: a stunt with real stakes, a narrator with a strong point of view, a contrarian "why it sucked" angle, or a new format (AI "time-travel vlogs").

If we make "What If You Were Einstein for a Day?" as a straight tour of his routine, we join the 7-view pile. The format only works if we bring a mechanic nobody else has. Section 4 proposes one.

**Where it lives: a new channel, same studio.**
- The Blink Files promises a space-powers serial. Borrowed Day promises a standalone search format.
- On one channel, the recommendation system can't learn who either audience is, and each upload confuses the other's subscribers.
- **Shared:** the rig, kit, score synth, linter and render pipeline.
- **Not shared:** the name, the narrator voice and the packaging style.
- **Don't run both at full weekly cadence** with one founder. That is exactly the cadence failure in `02-vc-pass-premortem.md`.
- Let the one-week Shorts test (§9) and Ep1's retention curve decide which one gets the weekly slot.
- Borrowed Day is also the standalone fallback the Blink Files pre-mortem already prescribed, so the work isn't wasted either way.

**Read this table carefully.** Even "What Nobody Tells You", the struggle side, is already slop vocabulary. Showing the hidden cost is necessary, but it is not a differentiator on its own.

### Four things the data says

1. **Roles beat names, unless the name *is* a wish.** The big numbers come from roles and conditions:
   - President, trillionaire, pro footballer, the richest man, an ant.
   - Named people only work when the person *stands for* a feeling. Cleopatra means power, gold and seduction. Elon means money.
   - **Einstein has no winner at all.** "Genius" can't be felt from the outside. Ifverse's *"What If You Had 500 IQ?"* (1.15M) worked because it turned genius into a *power you use*.
2. **The struggle angle works for history** (StickTory's "Why it sucked", 1M+), but only when it's specific and a little shocking, never as a moral.
3. **Live-action creators own football and money stunts.** They have real access. Animation can't compete on access. It can compete on **the moment no camera can reach**: the 94th-minute penalty from inside the player's head, or the barge arriving at Tarsus.
4. **Short and punchy won the old era** (Meet Arnold, 2–4 min). Today's winners run 9–16 min with a narrator. We fit the modern band (9–11 min, two mid-rolls).

## 3. The six topics, scored

**The Day Test.** A borrowed day needs five things:
- **Wish:** the best thing, readable in one frame in the first 10 seconds.
- **Body:** a physical sensation the viewer can almost feel.
- **Cost:** a hidden struggle that is *true and sourced*.
- **Peak:** a moment only this person ever gets.
- **Return:** what *they* would envy in *your* life (see §4).

| Borrowed day | Wish | Body | Cost | Peak | Return | Demand evidence | Legal risk | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The richest person on Earth** | 5 | 3 | 5 | 4 | 5 | Very high (3M, 4.9M, 10.8M) | Medium → **low if fictional** | **Pilot** |
| **The best footballer in the world** | 5 | 5 | 4 | 5 | 5 | Very high (4.5M, 3.7M, 18M) | Medium → **low if fictional** | **Ep 2** |
| **Cleopatra** | 5 | 4 | 5 | 5 | 5 | High (841K, 572K, 1.25M "sucked") | Low (public domain) | **Ep 3** |
| **An astronaut** | 4 | 5 | 4 | 5 | 5 | Medium, and the field is empty of quality | Low | **Ep 4**. Cheapest: the zero-g kit already exists |
| **The biggest movie star alive** | 4 | 2 | 4 | 3 | 5 | Low–medium (156K, 62K) | Medium → low if fictional | Later. Merge into "most famous person alive" |

**Next in line** (same test, high scores): Pharaoh, President of the most powerful country (fictional), K-pop idol, a Formula 1 driver, a Roman emperor, the world's best chef, a deep-sea explorer, a Viking king.

## 4. How we do it differently: the Return

Every competitor answers one question: *what is it like to be them?* Nobody answers the other half:

> **While you were living their day, they were living yours.**

Every episode ends at midnight with **the Return**. You wake up back in your own bed, and in 60–90 seconds you see what *they* did with your day:

| Borrowed day | What they did with yours (comedy) | The one thing they'd keep (the lump in the throat) |
|---|---|---|
| Richest person | Walked through the market and nobody wanted anything from him. Haggled for oranges and lost. | A friend who called "just to talk", who wanted nothing |
| Best footballer | Ate a whole plate of jollof with no nutritionist watching. Played a street match in flip-flops and lost 4–2. | Playing for fun, where nobody booed him |
| Cleopatra | Opened the fridge and found *ice*. Took two painkillers for a headache and cried. | The most powerful woman of her age couldn't buy what's in your kitchen cupboard |
| Astronaut | Stood in the rain for twenty minutes | Weight. Lying down and *feeling* the bed |
| Einstein | Opened the map on your phone, which only works because of his relativity (GPS clocks are corrected ~38 µs/day) | His own idea in a stranger's pocket, helping them find a bus stop |

### Why this is the moat
1. **It's an open loop planted in minute one** and paid off in the last 90 seconds: *"Somewhere, the real Cleopatra just woke up in your bed."* Viewers stay to the end to see it. That is retention by design, not by hope.
2. **It's the dopamine hit and the heart hit in one video.**
   - **The fantasy:** you get their life.
   - **The comedy:** they get yours.
   - **The heart:** your ordinary life turns out to be worth something.
   - Nobody walks away feeling lectured, because the message is never said. It's *shown* by a queen staring at an ice cube.
3. **It's cheap to make.** "Your life" is one recurring home set with a recurring family. That set is built once and reused every episode. Only the borrowed world is new, so production time drops per episode instead of rising (pre-mortem risk #2 in `02-vc-pass-premortem.md`).
4. **It can't be cloned by a prompt.** The Return needs an ensemble with history (your grandma, your little sister, the neighbour's goat). Their reactions to a different legend every week become a sitcom that rewards returning viewers.
5. **The thumbnail has a second hook nobody else has.**
   - Most thumbnails in the niche are one image (you on the throne).
   - Ours is a split frame: you on the throne, *and* Cleopatra in a plastic chair holding an ice cube like a jewel.
   - The curiosity gap is the second half.

### How "you" is shown (no confusion about bodies)
- **The borrowed day:** we see the famous person's body, because you're inside it. The narrator says "you".
- **The Return:** we see *your* body (the recurring "You" avatar) behaving like the famous person. Cleopatra-in-you sits like a queen on a plastic chair and points at the fridge for grapes, and Grandma isn't having it.
- The comedy comes from body language, not from text or explanation.

## 5. The episode shape (9–11 min)

| Time | Section | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | **The peak, first** | The best moment of their day, mid-action (the penalty run-up, the barge at Tarsus), then a freeze. *"This is your day. At midnight, you give it back."* |
| 0:30 | **Waking up** | The first physical sensation: silk, 400 missed calls, floating out of your sleeping bag. The B-loop gets planted: *"Somewhere, they just woke up in your bed."* |
| 1:00–4:00 | **The good hours** | Three escalating dopamine beats, each anchored to a sourced fact turned into a picture |
| ~4:00 | **The first crack** | The rule nobody sees (food tasters, the ice bath, the security team) → **mid-roll 1** |
| 4:30–7:00 | **The weight** | The struggle, specific and true, never preached. One decision only they can make. |
| 7:00–8:00 | **The peak, finished** | The cold open pays off with the full moment |
| 8:00 | **Midnight** | The swap snaps back → **mid-roll 2** lands just before the reveal |
| 8:30–9:45 | **The Return** | What they did with your day, and the one thing they'd keep |
| End | **The question** | *"Would you have given it back?"* Then tease the next borrowed day. |

The pacing linter rules stay: a beat every 45s, a hook in 15s, and mid-rolls only on open loops.

## 6. Pilot beat sheet: "What If You Were the Richest Person on Earth for One Day?"

The richest person is **fictional**: no real face, name or company. Facts about the real super-rich are used as *texture*, with sources in the description.

> Kade (the Blink Files' rocket billionaire) already exists in the rig. Using him links the two shows for anyone watching both, at zero extra cost. If we want this format fully standalone, give him a new face.

1. **Cold open.** You stand on a rocket gantry at dawn and press a button. The launch shakes your teeth. Freeze. *"This is your day. At midnight, you give it back."*
2. **Wake.** The bed is the size of your whole flat. A man is already standing at the door: your head of security. *"Somewhere, the real one just woke up on your mattress, under your fan, with your phone alarm going off."*
3. **Good hour 1, money as speed.** Your fortune earns more while you brush your teeth than most people earn in a year. Shown as a tap that pours gold coins the whole time you brush, overflowing the sink. *(At $400B and 5% a year, that's ~$38,000 a minute.)*
4. **Good hour 2, yes to everything.** Breakfast from any country, a jet before lunch. Every request gets a yes.
5. **Good hour 3, the call.** A president calls *you*.
6. **First crack.** The guard won't let you walk to the shop. *Real texture:* one tech company spent about **$27M** in a year protecting its CEO (Meta proxy filing, 2025). → **Mid-roll 1**
7. **The weight, part 1.** Everyone smiles at you, and every smile wants something. An old school friend calls, and halfway through, the ask arrives.
8. **The weight, part 2.** Your phone holds 400 decisions and 4,000 jobs depend on them. You haven't eaten sitting down.
9. **The peak.** The launch from the cold open finishes. It's the most beautiful thing you've ever seen, and there's no one next to you to turn to.
10. **Midnight.** The swap snaps back. → **Mid-roll 2**
11. **The Return.**
    - The richest person spent your day haggling for oranges and losing.
    - He ate with your family off one plate and let your little sister win at cards.
    - He sat on the bus and nobody knew him.
    - The last image: he's at your window, at midnight, not wanting to leave.
12. **The question.** *"Would you have given it back?"* Then a glimpse of tomorrow's borrowed day: a stadium roaring.

## 7. Guardrails

- **No real living people.**
  - The richest person, the footballer and the movie star are fictional.
  - No real names, faces, club crests, kits or brand logos.
  - Inventing private struggles for a real living person is defamation risk. It also falls under YouTube's synthetic-content disclosure rules for realistic depictions of real people.
- **Historical figures are real, and so are their facts.**
  - Every claim about Cleopatra or Einstein carries a source in the description. Example: Plutarch, *Life of Antony* 27: she rarely needed an interpreter, and she was the first of her dynasty to learn Egyptian.
  - Popular myths (the donkey-milk bath, the asp) are either avoided or shown *as* myths.
- **Original by construction.**
  - Original characters, procedural score, a human-written script, and a format mechanic (the Return) nobody else uses.
  - That is the defence against YouTube's "inauthentic content" demonetisation, which targets exactly the templated "for a day" uploads in §2's graveyard.

## 8. VC pass and pre-mortem

**The pass:** *"The niche is real but it's a land-grab that's already being lost to volume. Your edge is a story mechanic, and story mechanics don't show up in a title or thumbnail until someone has already clicked. Show me that the split-frame thumbnail out-clicks a plain one before you spend eight weeks building Alexandria."*

**It's April 2028. Borrowed Day failed. The post-mortem:**
1. **We picked names, not wishes.**
   - The Einstein and Mozart episodes looked prestigious and drew 20K.
   - The algorithm learned the channel was "history lessons", and the money and football episodes never recovered their reach.
2. **The struggle turned into a sermon.**
   - "Money can't buy happiness" was said out loud in Ep 3.
   - Retention fell off a cliff at the first crack (−35% in 30 seconds). The comments said "preachy".
3. **The Return got too long.** It grew from 90s to 3 minutes because it was the writers' favourite part. Viewers who came for Cleopatra's palace felt bait-and-switched.
4. **Every borrowed world was built from scratch.** Alexandria, a stadium, the ISS, a Hollywood lot. The home set was reused but the expensive half wasn't, and cadence slipped to fortnightly.
5. **The narrator voice was generic.**
   - In a second-person format, the voice *is* the brand: Hypothetically wins on persona.
   - A stock TTS voice made us sound like the graveyard.
6. **A real footballer's lookalike drew a complaint.** The design was "fictional", but the haircut and number weren't.

**What we change now so this doesn't happen:**
- Pick days by the **Day Test**, never by fame.
- **The cost is shown, never stated.** `npm run lint:episode` now fails any line like "money can't buy…", "the truth is…" or "be grateful".
- **Hard cap on the Return:** 90 seconds.
- Build borrowed worlds as **reusable kits**: the stadium serves football, concerts and a presidential rally; the palace serves Cleopatra, a pharaoh and an emperor.
- **Cast the narrator voice before Ep 1.** Run a blind listening test against Hypothetically and two graveyard channels.
- **The design rule for fictional people:** they must differ from any real person on 3 of 5 traits (face shape, hair, skin, build, number/kit).

## 9. The single assumption we haven't tested

> **That viewers want to *be* a great person, rather than to *feel* one thing that person has.**

Your list mixes people (Cleopatra, Einstein) with roles (astronaut, footballer, movie star, the richest man). Every big number in §2 comes from a role or from a person who *stands for* a wish. Einstein has no winner at all.

If this assumption is wrong, the plan of "live the lives of great people" picks the wrong episodes. The format survives, but the slate doesn't.

### The cheapest test: one week, no full episodes
1. **Make six 30–40s Shorts.** Each is a cold open only: the peak moment, the freeze, and *"At midnight, you give it back."* Make one per topic: richest person, footballer, Cleopatra, astronaut, movie star, Einstein's brain.
2. **Post them on the same weekday at the same time.** Compare the **viewed vs. swiped-away** ratio and the 48-hour views.
3. **Thumbnail test.** On the pilot, run YouTube **Test & Compare** with a split-frame thumbnail (the throne and the ice cube) against a single-image one. If the split frame doesn't win, the Return still works as a story device but not as packaging, and that changes how we sell every episode.
4. **Decision rule.**
   - Build long-form only for the top 3 Shorts.
   - If "Einstein's brain" lands in the bottom two, the "great people" slate becomes a "great *days*" slate.

## Production rules carried over from the founder's review of Ep1

These apply to every episode from now on, Blink Files and Borrowed Day alike.

1. **Write for the viewer, never for the writers' room.**
   - Nothing from our planning conversations appears on screen.
   - No slogans, themes or rules painted on props.
2. **No words on vehicles or houses.**
   - Buses, houses and shopfronts are wordless. Signboards are painted shapes and colours.
   - Removed from the shared kit: "NO CONDITION IS PERMANENT" (bus) and "THIS HOUSE IS NOT FOR SALE" (house).
3. **The only words allowed in frame are words that would really be there**, and that the story needs: a business card, a phone screen, a scoreboard. `npx tsx scripts/audit-text.ts` lists every on-screen string and flags anything that reads like a slogan or caption.
4. **Heroes don't scowl.**
   - `determined`, `serious` and `confused` no longer furrow the brows into a V, which read as anger on Jonah.
   - Focus is now shown with level brows; puzzlement with one raised brow.
   - Furrowed brows are kept for `smug`, the villain register.

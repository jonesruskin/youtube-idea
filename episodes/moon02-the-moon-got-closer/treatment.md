# Treatment · moon02 · The Moon Got Closer

> Checked by `npx tsx scripts/check-treatment.ts moon02` before anything is built (docs/00 S13–S16).

## Title
The Moon Got Closer Every Night. Day 1 to Day 200 by the Sea

## Premise
A black hole smaller than a grain of sand but a third as heavy as the Moon drifts in from deep space and gets caught between the Earth and the Moon. It cannot be seen. Every loop it makes around the Moon works like a spacecraft's gravity slingshot in reverse: the visitor gains a little speed and the Moon loses a little, and a slower Moon sinks closer to the Earth. The tides grow with every loop. In a harbour town, ten-year-old Wren, her mother (who runs the lifeboat) and her grandmother Nana Odile (who keeps the family's 140-year tide log) move uphill one street at a time. After two hundred days the visitor has stolen enough speed to escape, and it leaves. The Moon stays where it was dropped: three times closer, for good.

## Hook (0:00–0:30)
Night. A harbour street. The Moon rises over the sea wall three times too big, close enough to see its mountains, and the sea climbs the street after it, lifting parked cars, while a girl in pyjamas watches from an upstairs window. "The first night, we thought it was beautiful."

## Clock
Unit: kilometres to the Moon (with the day and the height of high tide)
1. Day 1: 384,000 km. High tide is 40 centimetres over the table; the fish market floods to the ankle.
2. Day 30: 330,000 km. The Moon is visibly bigger; the sea wall is overtopped; the lower street is abandoned.
3. Day 60: 290,000 km. Tides have doubled; the harbour board still says it will settle. The month is now 18 days long.
4. Day 90: 250,000 km. Tides are 4 times normal; the lighthouse is an island twice a day.
5. Day 120: 210,000 km. Tides are 6 times normal; half the town is gone at high water; the ground itself rises 2 metres with each pass.
6. Day 150: 170,000 km. Tides are 11 times normal; the Moon is twice as wide and bright enough to read by.
7. Day 180: 140,000 km. Tides are 20 times normal; the sea reaches the church steps on the hill.
8. Day 200: 128,000 km. The visitor leaves. The Moon stops. Tides are 27 times normal; a month lasts 5 days.

## Mystery
Question: Why is Nana Odile moving everything she owns up the hill at night, when the water has only reached the doorstep?
Owner: Nana Odile. On Day 9 her pencil arithmetic told her how high the sea would finally come; the harbour board had just promised the town it would settle, and the last time she warned them about a flood they called her a silly old woman.
Plants:
1. Day 3: she locks the tide log in the bread bin and writes in it only after Wren is in bed.
2. Day 12: Wren finds the deeds to the ruined chapel on the hill in Nana's coat, bought the week before for almost nothing.
3. Day 30: every forecast on the news is beaten within days; Nana never looks surprised, and she has already moved the beds.
4. Day 60: Nana paints a white line on the chapel steps and will not say what it is for.
Answer: The white line is where her Day 9 sum said the last high tide would stop. She was not hiding the log out of fear; she was getting the hill ready for the whole town, and the line is where the sea stops on Day 200, to the step.

## Twists
1. We thought the Moon was falling towards us, but nothing falls that slowly: something we cannot see is stealing its speed, one loop at a time.
2. We thought the scientists could find the visitor with telescopes, but it is a black hole smaller than a grain of sand, and the only instrument big enough to detect it is the Moon itself.
3. We thought the Moon would hit the Earth, but it never can: a moon that came close enough would be pulled apart long before it touched, and ours will stop far short of that.
4. We thought Nana was losing her mind on the hill at night, but she had worked out the final tide on Day 9 and was building the town's refuge alone.
5. We thought Nana bought the roofless chapel because her sums told her to, but it is where her mother carried her up the hill in the January 1953 flood: the one place the sea never reached.
6. We thought the visitor would take the Moon away with it or drop it on us, but every loop made the visitor faster until it flew off into the dark, and the Moon stayed exactly where it was left.

## Second storyline
January 1953, sepia memory film with its own music box: Nana at seven holds the torch while her mother Rose writes the tide; Rose warns the Harbour Board and is told "it will settle"; the real North Sea surge of 31 January 1953 comes over the quay in the dark; Rose carries her up to the chapel. It explains the book, the Board's words, and the chapel, and pays off as twist 5.

## Science
1. The pull that raises tides grows with the cube of closeness: halve the Moon's distance and the tides are eight times higher. — Source: Newton, Principia (1687); NOAA Tides and Water Levels
2. We measure the Moon's distance to within centimetres by bouncing lasers off mirrors the Apollo astronauts left there; it normally drifts away 3.8 cm a year. — Source: NASA Lunar Laser Ranging
3. A closer moon orbits faster: at a third of the distance, a month lasts about five days. — Source: Kepler's third law (1619)
4. A gravity slingshot trades speed between a small body and a big one; spacecraft like Voyager used it to gain speed from planets. — Source: NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory
5. A black hole a third as heavy as the Moon would be smaller than a grain of sand and completely dark; such primordial black holes are a real, unconfirmed hypothesis. — Source: Hawking (1971); Scholtz and Unwin (2020)
6. The shape of a bay can multiply a tide: the Bay of Fundy's reach 16 metres today. — Source: Canadian Hydrographic Service
7. On the night of 31 January 1953 a North Sea storm surge flooded the east coast of England; 307 people died in England and more than 1,800 in the Netherlands. — Source: Met Office, The 1953 East Coast Flood
7. A moon that comes closer than about 18,000 km to the Earth is torn apart by tides (the Roche limit); that is how rings form. — Source: Édouard Roche (1848)
8. The solid ground rises and falls about 30 centimetres twice a day with the Moon, and nobody feels it. — Source: International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service

## Heart
Want: Wren wants her street, her grandmother and the sea she grew up beside to stay as they are.
Cost: She loses the street and the harbour, and has to see that the person everyone laughed at was the only one telling the truth.
Payoff: On Day 200 the last tide stops on Nana's white line, the town is standing behind it, and Nana hands Wren the pencil: "New book. Page one."

## Final image
A year later: the rebuilt town high on the hill, a Moon three times too big over a sea that climbs thirty metres and falls again, and a new tide log open on the chapel wall in a child's handwriting: "Day 1. High tide on time."

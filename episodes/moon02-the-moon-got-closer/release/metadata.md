THE REAL SCIENCE IN THIS STORY
(1) The Moon’s distance is measured to within centimetres by timing laser pulses reflected from mirrors left by the Apollo astronauts; it normally recedes about 3.8 cm a year. — Source: NASA, Lunar Laser Ranging experiment
(2) The tide-raising pull of the Moon grows with the cube of its closeness: at half the distance, tides are eight times higher. — Source: Newton, Principia (1687); NOAA Tides and Water Levels
(3) A closer moon orbits faster (Kepler’s third law): at 330,000 km a month lasts about 22 days; at a third of the distance, about five. — Source: Kepler, Harmonices Mundi (1619)
(4) In a gravity assist, a small body swinging past a larger one trades speed with it; spacecraft such as Voyager gained speed this way from the planets. — Source: NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory
(5) A black hole with a third of the Moon’s mass would be about 0.04 mm across and give off no light; such primordial black holes are a real but unconfirmed hypothesis. — Source: Hawking (1971); Scholtz and Unwin (2020)
(6) The shape of a bay can multiply a tide: the Bay of Fundy in Canada already has tides of up to 16 metres. — Source: Canadian Hydrographic Service
(7) A moon closer than about 18,000 km to the Earth would be pulled apart by tides (the Roche limit); that is how planetary rings form. — Source: Édouard Roche (1848)
(8) The solid Earth has a tide of its own: the ground rises and falls about 30 cm twice a day, and with the Moon at 210,000 km that becomes nearly two metres. — Source: International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service

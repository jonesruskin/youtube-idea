THE REAL SCIENCE IN THIS STORY
(1) Venus near the far side of the Sun: sunlight needs ~361 s to reach it and ~860 s more to cross 1.72 AU back to us, versus 499 s straight from the Sun, so it fades ~12 minutes after the Sun (6:52 → 7:04). — Source: Calculation from 1 AU = 149.6 million km, c = 299,792 km/s, Venus orbit 0.72 AU (NASA JPL planetary fact sheets)
(2) Jupiter is 5.2 AU from the Sun; light needs ~43 minutes to reach it and ~37 more to reach us when it is 4.5 AU away: about 72 minutes after the Sun. — Source: NASA Jupiter fact sheet (nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov)
(3) The Sun’s tide-raising force is about 46% of the Moon’s; at spring tide the two add up, so without the Sun the tidal range would fall by roughly a third. — Source: NOAA Ocean Service, ‘Tides and Water Levels’ tutorial
(4) Water holds far more heat than air, so the sea cools months more slowly than the land; even when ice forms later, it floats and keeps the water below it liquid. — Source: National Snow and Ice Data Center, ‘All About Sea Ice’; NOAA, ‘Ocean heat content’
(5) Anything that absorbs starlight must give off waste heat as infrared; this is how astronomers search for Dyson spheres. — Source: Freeman Dyson, ‘Search for Artificial Stellar Sources of Infrared Radiation’, Science (1960)
(6) Coronal mass ejections take roughly 1 to 4 days to reach Earth, so particles already on their way would still arrive after the light was gone. — Source: NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center, ‘Coronal Mass Ejections’
(7) Estimates for a sunless Earth: around −18 °C average after a week, around −73 °C after a year. — Source: Live Science, ‘What would happen if the sun disappeared?’
(8) Burning fuel indoors produces carbon monoxide, which is colourless and odourless; it is a leading cause of winter poisonings. — Source: US CDC, ‘Carbon Monoxide Poisoning’
(9) A few metres underground the temperature stays close to the yearly average (about 10–16 °C in many places), whatever the weather. — Source: US Department of Energy, ‘Geothermal Heat Pumps’
(10) Montréal’s underground city (RÉSO) links about 32 km of tunnels; Helsinki has a large network of civil-defence shelters dug into bedrock. — Source: Tourisme Montréal, ‘The Underground City (RÉSO)’; City of Helsinki civil defence shelters
(11) Satellites have measured the Sun's total brightness continuously since 1978. It naturally varies by about 0.1% over the 11-year solar cycle, so a screen that blocked a hundred-thousandth of its light would be lost in the noise. — Source: NASA, ‘Solar Irradiance Datasets’ (scdi.smce.nasa.gov/solar_data_access.html); G. Kopp, Living Reviews in Solar Physics (2025)
(12) HD 219134 is a real orange dwarf star about 21 light-years away in Cassiopeia, with at least five known planets. — Source: NASA Exoplanet Archive, ‘HD 219134’; Motalebi et al., Astronomy & Astrophysics (2015)
(13) In 1974 the Arecibo radio telescope sent a message of 1,679 pulses toward the star cluster M13, because 1,679 = 23 × 73 (two primes) can only be laid out as one grid, which forms a picture. — Source: Cornell University / SETI Institute, ‘The Arecibo Message’ (1974)
(14) Radio waves travel at the speed of light, so a signal to a star 21.3 light-years away and an answer back take about 43 years: 1983 → 2004 → 2026. — Source: Definition of the light-year (IAU); HD 219134 distance 6.5 pc (Gaia DR3)
(15) A ship near light speed cannot carry enough fuel to stop; one proposed way is to brake a light sail against the destination star’s light. Here the shell focuses the whole Sun onto the sail. — Source: Heller & Hippke, ‘Deceleration of high-velocity interstellar photon sails into bound orbits at Alpha Centauri’, Astrophysical Journal Letters (2017)

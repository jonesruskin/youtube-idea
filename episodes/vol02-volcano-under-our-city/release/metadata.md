THE REAL SCIENCE IN THIS STORY
(1) Volcanic unrest under a monogenetic field begins with swarms of small earthquakes as magma forces its way up through the crust; under Auckland they would be felt and located once the magma is within roughly 10–30 km of the surface. — Source: Auckland Lifelines Forum (2007); GNS Science, ‘Auckland Volcanic Field’
(2) Auckland sits on a volcanic field of about 53 volcanoes; each erupted once (except Rangitoto), so the next eruption will most likely open at a brand-new site. — Source: Hayward, ‘Volcanoes of Auckland’ (Auckland University Press, 2019)
(3) Small earthquakes in the Auckland Volcanic Field are hard to record and locate because of the high level of surface noise in a city. — Source: GNS Science / GeoNet, Auckland Volcanic Field monitoring
(4) In New Zealand the advice in an earthquake is Drop, Cover and Hold: get down, take cover under a sturdy table, hold on until the shaking stops. — Source: NZ ShakeOut / National Emergency Management Agency
(5) New Zealand Civil Defence advises every household to keep a grab bag ready: water, food, torch, radio, medicines, copies of documents. — Source: National Emergency Management Agency (NZ), Get Ready
(6) As magma rises, gases (mostly CO₂) escape ahead of it. CO₂ is heavier than air, so it collects in hollows, basements and low ponds, where it can suffocate animals; springs can warm and fizz. — Source: USGS Volcano Hazards Program, ‘Volcanic gases’
(7) Magma pushing toward the surface lifts the ground above it, measurable by GPS stations and satellites as centimetres or more of uplift. — Source: USGS, ‘Monitoring ground deformation’
(8) In exercise scenarios for Auckland, the evacuation area changes over time as uncertainty about the vent location shrinks; the expected initial danger zone is about 5 km around the vent. — Source: Exercise Ruaumoko studies, Bulletin of Volcanology (2012); Science Learning Hub, ‘Exercise Ruaumoko’
(9) Auckland’s volcanic contingency plans use contraflow on the motorways and public buses to move people out of an evacuation zone quickly. — Source: Auckland Emergency Management, Volcanic Contingency Plan
(10) Ash from Rangitoto’s eruption (about 600 years ago) buried a Māori settlement on Motutapu Island; footprints of people and dogs walking through the wet ash are preserved, and there is no evidence anyone there was killed. — Source: Hayward, ‘Volcanoes of Auckland’ (2019); Nichol, New Zealand Journal of Geology and Geophysics (1982)
(11) Rangitoto rose out of the sea in the Hauraki Gulf about 600 years ago in two phases; it is the youngest and largest volcano in the Auckland field, now covered in pōhutukawa forest. — Source: Hayward, ‘Volcanoes of Auckland’ (2019); Needham et al., Journal of Volcanology and Geothermal Research (2011)
(12) Sound travels about 343 metres per second in air, so a sound 8 km away arrives about 23 seconds after the light. — Source: NIST / standard speed of sound in air at 20 °C
(13) When rising magma meets groundwater it flashes it to steam and explodes; the first eruptions of an Auckland volcano would send base surges, ground-hugging clouds of ash and rock, racing out for kilometres. — Source: Science Learning Hub, ‘Future explosions in Auckland’; Natural Hazards Commission (2025)
(14) After the first explosive phase, Auckland eruptions typically build a scoria cone from lava fountains and may send slow lava flows; the whole eruption lasts weeks to months, then the vent never erupts again. — Source: Hayward, ‘Volcanoes of Auckland’ (2019)
